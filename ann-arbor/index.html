Getting More Years From Your Water Heater in Ann Arbor

Many early failures trace back to maintenance that never happened, and the habits that prevent them are simple. Drain and flush the tank every year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and test the relief valve each year so it doesn't seize. Keep the temperature near 120 degrees for safer water and less scale. For a tankless heater in a hard-water area, plan on descaling once a year so the heat exchanger stays clean.

Equipment age plays a big role. With regular maintenance, a tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts acting up, like discolored hot water, rumbling, or moisture under the tank, it's smart to start planning a replacement. A planned replacement is cheaper and is far less stressful than scrambling for a new heater with water spreading across the floor.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Take a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, inexpensive repair, but if nobody replaces it, the water starts eating the tank itself, and just like that a minor fix has become a full replacement. That's why we encourage homeowners to save our number in their phone and reach out at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves money and spares you a flooded utility room.

Common Water Heater Problems in Ann Arbor

Water heaters in and around Ann Arbor deal with a familiar set of stresses. Depending on whether a home is on municipal water or a private well, minerals can be part of the picture: dissolved minerals drop out as sediment on the tank floor, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the popping and rumbling many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why annual descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

We also see a lot of aging equipment. A tank-style water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, and by then the anode rod is often used up, leaving the steel open to rust. The area has a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Replacing an older heater frequently means bringing the install up to current code: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a T&P discharge line that terminates correctly, a flue that drafts correctly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Well water brings its own challenges, such as heavier sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that occasionally develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early.
